# Short Chinese conversation video

Read `../../../docs/workflow/video-editing-workflow.md` and `../video-reference-research/analysis.md` for the shared workflow, observed style and evidence limits. For a specific class, resolve offering, textbook and full lesson key; inspect current source, approval state and applicable project workflow before drafting. Obtain the required content approval before generating visuals when the project requires it. A video reference is not a source for lesson content.

1. Define audience, level, communicative situation and the target phrases from the actual lesson. Write a short, coherent exchange with clear speakers and a meaningful outcome; check Chinese, pinyin, translation and pronunciation against the class requirements. Preserve the required character variant. Do not invent textbook authority.
2. Write a compact treatment and turn-by-turn edit map: learning outcome, opening and ending; speaker, exact dialogue, pronunciation, caption and optional translation, source/asset, shot, in/out, sound cue and any student response window. Use actual approved copy. Give each shot a comprehension or story purpose. Decide aspect ratio for the destination; vertical 1080×1920 is a starting point for phone use, not a classroom requirement. A storyboard or rendered style frame is optional when the look is new, continuity is uncertain, or the user requests it.
3. Establish the location and both speakers in a clear two-shot. Either hold a coherent scene with subtle expressions and gestures, or cut to the active speaker where it helps comprehension. Keep characters, setting and props consistent across angles. Do not force zooms or transitions; a clean cut or a stable shot is sufficient. Do not copy reference artwork, logos or soundtrack.
4. Time each readable caption to its spoken turn. Use high contrast and safe margins; highlight only meaningful words. Apply pinyin and Vietnamese only as required by the specific course and space, keeping essential language visible and legible. Do not remove words, reveal answers early, or confuse subtitles with a complete oral practice task. Provide a clear prompt and time if students must respond.
5. Use authorized voices/assets; check each spoken syllable, tone, speaker assignment and caption against approved dialogue. Prefer intelligible speech over music. Character animation and lip sync are optional and must match the delivered result; do not claim them if absent.
6. Use existing HyperFrames for editable timed compositions when suited; use FFmpeg/ffprobe for media checks and encoding. Read the installed HyperFrames skill for its actual render contract; confirm runtime compatibility. Use an appropriate existing editor for footage, character animation or audio where needed. Code-directed frame-by-frame visuals, including methods demonstrated by [Lemo-Opuscar](https://github.com/lemomo-ai/lemo-opuscar), are optional; check their renderer, runtime and handoff fit before choosing them. Do not install its plugin or dependencies just to consult its directing and style references. Keep sources and editable project with the output.
7. Review the final export at normal speed with sound and at intended phone/classroom size. Compare it with the treatment and edit map. Check every line, pronunciation, caption timing, speaker, crop, contrast, visual continuity, pause, sound cue, black or missing frame and ending. Inspect a contact sheet or key frames as a supplement to watching the export; check ffprobe dimensions, duration and audio. Record checks and unresolved issues; technical render is not teacher approval, classroom playback or release.

Positive routing: a one-scene Chinese airport exchange; a short café role-play; edit a short dialogue clip for a lesson. Negative routing: a long multi-scene lesson (use chinese-conversation-long-video); a general social ad (use the relevant social skill); an in-person slide deck (use project slide workflow).
